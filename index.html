Business development

Growth starts with the right problem

To me, business development is the work of finding where a real customer challenge meets a solution that genuinely fits — and then building the relationships that let both sides grow. Having worked on the technology side, I look at opportunities with a practical question in mind: will this actually work for the customer?

  1. Business development strategy

    Deciding where to focus — which markets, segments and offers deserve time — instead of chasing every lead.

  2. Market research & opportunity identification

    Understanding industries, buyers and their pressures well enough to see opportunities before they become tenders.

  3. Lead generation & qualification

    Building a steady flow of conversations, then being honest about which ones have a real need, budget and timing.

  4. Strategic partnerships & ecosystems

    Working with vendors, distributors, integrators and complementary partners so customers get a complete solution.

  5. Relationship development

    Growth that lasts comes from trust: following through, adding value between deals, and thinking long term.

  6. From customer challenge to technology solution

    Translating a business problem into the right mix of technology — and knowing when the honest answer is “not yet”.

Questions I ask before chasing an opportunity

  1. Who owns the problem, and what is it costing them today?
  2. Why would they act now rather than next year?
  3. What does success look like in their words, not ours?
  4. Which partners do we need to deliver it well?
  5. Is this a relationship worth building beyond one deal?

Simple questions — but answering them honestly saves months of effort.

Technology sales · IT sales

Sell the outcome, not the specification

Technology sales is one of the most valuable — and most misunderstood — skills in IT. The best technology salespeople aren't the ones who know the most features; they're the ones who understand the customer's business and can explain how a solution changes it. These are the ideas I find myself sharing most with young professionals, sales teams and IT companies.

Feature-based selling

  • Starts with the product and its datasheet
  • Talks about specifications, licences and speeds
  • Competes mainly on price
  • Ends the conversation when the demo ends
“Our firewall handles 10 Gbps of throughput.”

Value-based selling

  • Starts with the customer's business and its risks
  • Talks about outcomes, costs avoided and time saved
  • Competes on fit, trust and results
  • Builds a relationship that continues after the deal
“Your branches stay online and protected during peak season — without adding staff.”

Technology sales fundamentals

How IT buying really works: multiple stakeholders, technical and financial approvals, long cycles, and why trust matters more than pressure.

Discovery conversations

Asking questions that uncover the real need — the business pain behind the technical request.

  • What triggered this project now?
  • What happens if nothing changes?
  • Who else is affected, and who decides?

Qualifying leads & opportunities

Separating interest from intent: need, budget, authority, timing, and whether you can genuinely win and deliver.

Technical solutions in business language

Turning architecture diagrams into stories a CFO or CEO cares about — risk, cost, growth, compliance and customer experience.

Consultative selling & objection handling

Acting as an adviser rather than a vendor, and treating objections as information — usually about risk, budget or timing — rather than as rejection.

Managing the sales pipeline

Keeping an honest pipeline from lead to qualified opportunity to closed deal: clear stages, next steps on every deal, and the discipline to drop the ones that won't close.

Sales skills across IT solutions

Each technology area has its own buyers, language and concerns. Knowing what each buyer really worries about is half of the conversation.

Cloud

Cost predictability, migration risk, performance and the skills to run it afterwards.

Cybersecurity

Business risk, regulatory pressure and resilience — not just tools and threat lists.

Infrastructure

Uptime, lifecycle cost, scalability and how new systems fit what is already there.

Software & SaaS

User adoption, integration with existing systems and a clear return on the subscription.

Data & AI

Realistic use cases, data readiness, governance and measurable results from pilots.

Managed services

Service levels, accountability and freeing internal teams to focus on the business.
